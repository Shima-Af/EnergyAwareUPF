                                        Abstract
    Energy-aware control of softwarised mobile networks requires more information than
current telemetry alone provides. A controller must estimate the power attributable to an
individual network function, anticipate traffic demand, and compare the performance of
alternative configurations before selecting one. This thesis investigates how measurement-
grounded artificial intelligence can supply these additional quantities through augmented
observability, using the selection of User Plane Function (UPF) implementations in 5G
networks as its experimental application.
    The proposed pipeline connects physical profiling, cluster-level traffic forecasting,
digital-twin evaluation and shared-policy reinforcement learning. Profiling of two UPF
deployments provides the measurements used to fit power and performance surrogates. A
cluster-first forecasting workflow converts mobile-network traffic and base-station geog-
raphy into predictions at the service-area scale required by the controller. It predicts four
15-minute horizons, with the first aligned to control. A digital twin combines load inputs
with the surrogates and activation-cost accounting to evaluate candidate UPF choices.
Shared policies pool experience across sites, while a controlled comparison examines the
additional contribution of a centralised training critic.
    The results show that forecasting the aggregated service-area signal achieves 0.21–0.23
skill over persistence under the tested fixed-budget comparison, whereas summing per-
station forecasts provides no improvement over persistence. Across eight training seeds,
a shared-policy controller (MAPPO) outperforms independently trained per-site learners
and reduces the combined cost by 10.2 % relative to the test-tuned hysteresis reference.
Its advantage over Shared-PPO is modest and concerns the balance between energy and
model-predicted QoS penalties; the comparison does not establish an energy advantage
over Shared-PPO. Re-evaluation places MAPPO first by reward among six controllers in
eight of ten alternative surrogate calibrations, while boundary-focused validation identifies
uncertainty in the predicted safe operating region.
    These findings demonstrate the utility of an integrated augmented-observability
pipeline within the studied modelled conditions. They also identify the measure-
ments needed to assess transfer to physical operation. The resulting contribution is a
measurement-grounded framework for connecting inferred network state to control deci-
sions, with a clear separation between performance in a calibrated twin and validation of
a live deployment. The 5G study provides a basis for investigating this approach in future
6G networks.

Keywords: augmented observability; user plane function; energy-aware control; network
digital twin; traffic forecasting; shared-policy reinforcement learning; 5G core networks.

                                       Riassunto
    Il controllo energeticamente efficiente delle reti mobili basate su funzioni software
richiede informazioni ulteriori rispetto a quelle fornite dalla sola telemetria. Un con-
trollore deve stimare la potenza attribuibile a una singola funzione di rete, anticipare
la domanda di traffico e confrontare le prestazioni di configurazioni alternative prima di
sceglierne una. Questa tesi studia come l’intelligenza artificiale ancorata alle misure possa
fornire tali informazioni attraverso l’osservabilità aumentata, utilizzando la selezione di
implementazioni della User Plane Function (UPF) nelle reti 5G come applicazione speri-
mentale.
    La pipeline proposta collega profilazione fisica, previsione del traffico per cluster,
valutazione mediante digital twin e apprendimento per rinforzo con politiche condivise.
La profilazione di due deployment UPF fornisce le misure per costruire modelli surrogati
di potenza e prestazioni. La previsione cluster-first trasforma il traffico della rete mobile
e la geografia delle stazioni radio in previsioni alla scala delle aree di servizio richiesta
dal controllore. Sono previsti quattro orizzonti di 15 minuti, il primo dei quali è allineato
al controllo. Un digital twin combina il carico con i surrogati e la contabilizzazione
dei costi di attivazione per valutare le alternative UPF. Le politiche condivise aggregano
l’esperienza dei siti, mentre un confronto controllato esamina il contributo aggiuntivo di
un critico centralizzato durante l’addestramento.
    I risultati mostrano che prevedere il segnale aggregato dell’area di servizio raggiunge
uno skill di 0.21–0.23 rispetto alla persistenza nel confronto a budget fissato, mentre
sommare le previsioni delle singole stazioni non supera il riferimento di persistenza.
Su otto seed di addestramento, un controllore a politica condivisa (MAPPO) supera gli
agenti indipendenti addestrati per sito e riduce il costo combinato del 10.2 % rispetto al
riferimento a isteresi ottimizzato sul test. Il vantaggio rispetto a Shared-PPO è modesto e
riguarda il bilanciamento tra energia e penalità di QoS previste dal modello; il confronto
non dimostra un vantaggio energetico rispetto a Shared-PPO. La rivalutazione colloca
MAPPO al primo posto per ricompensa tra sei controllori in otto delle dieci calibrazioni
alternative dei surrogati. La validazione vicino alla soglia di QoS evidenzia incertezza
nella regione operativa sicura prevista dal modello.
    Questi risultati dimostrano l’utilità della pipeline integrata nelle condizioni modellate
e identificano le misure necessarie per valutarne il trasferimento al funzionamento fisico.
Il contributo è un quadro metodologico ancorato alle misure che collega lo stato di rete
inferito alle decisioni di controllo, distinguendo le prestazioni nel twin calibrato dalla
validazione di un deployment reale. Lo studio 5G fornisce una base per investigare questo
approccio nelle future reti 6G.

Parole chiave: osservabilità aumentata; user plane function; controllo energeticamente
efficiente; digital twin di rete; previsione del traffico; apprendimento per rinforzo con
politiche condivise; reti core 5G.

                                                                Introduction

1.1     Motivation
Softwarised mobile networks expose an increasing number of configuration choices, in-
cluding the implementation and placement of the functions that forward user traffic.
Choosing among these alternatives requires an estimate of their energy and service con-
sequences under changing demand. For the User Plane Function (upf), a lower-power
implementation may be attractive at light load but unable to sustain the same operating
range as a higher-throughput implementation. The useful choice therefore depends on
both the condition of the network and the consequences of changing configuration.
    Telemetry provides only part of the information needed for this decision. Hardware
energy counters describe processor domains rather than individual network functions [1];
function-level energy accounting therefore requires attribution, consistent with the estima-
tion approach described in 3GPP performance measurements [2]. Packet loss and delay
can be measured for an active deployment, but those measurements do not reveal the
performance an inactive alternative would provide under the same load. Future demand is
also unavailable when a decision is taken. These gaps motivate models that complement
telemetry with attributed, predictive and counterfactual information.


1.2     Augmented observability: the central idea
In this thesis, augmented observability denotes the construction of measurement-grounded
estimates that extend the information available to a network controller. The term is used
operationally: it concerns which quantities can be estimated, how reliably they can be
estimated, and whether they support useful decisions. The work evaluates these properties
empirically; it does not claim a formal state-observability result for the network dynamics.
    The central research question is whether these estimates can be connected into a consis-
tent measurement-to-control pipeline for energy-aware upf selection, and what evidence

1.3 – Problem statement and scope                                                           3


     Profiling characterises the recorded deployments and provides the measurements used
to fit attributed-power and performance models. Forecasting supplies service-area demand
for four 15-minute horizons, with the first aligned to the controller’s decision interval. The
digital twin evaluates candidate upf choices by combining load inputs with the fitted
models and transition accounting. These modules organise or infer information for a
decision.
     The controller module evaluates the use of that information and compares learning
designs. Policy sharing pools experience during training: sites use the same fitted actor,
while each decision depends on the local observation. Independent and shared actors
receive the same fourteen local quantities. The experiments examine performance and
variation across sites, and a shared-actor comparison tests local and centralised training
critics. Establishing transfer specifically from data-rich to data-poor sites would require a
separate experiment that controls the available training data.
     The architecture is relevant to management-domain analytics, network-data analytics,
digital twins and closed-loop orchestration [3, 4, 5, 6]. These functions provide the context
discussed in Chapter 2; the thesis evaluates the measurement and decision methods rather
than claiming a deployed standards integration.


1.3     Problem statement and scope
The empirical case is energy-aware upf realisation selection. At each site and each 15-
minute interval, a controller selects a sd-core dpdk-based kernel-bypass data plane or a
user-space realisation (usr-upf). The recorded deployments exhibit different power and
performance characteristics: the former has a near-constant attributed-power floor and
supports higher load, while the latter offers lower attributed power at light load within
a narrower safe operating region. Selection must balance energy, QoS penalties and
switching costs under imperfect demand forecasts.
    The evidence combines physical measurements from the two 5G UPF deployments
with traffic derived from the Lyon traces. Controller evaluation is offline against a
measurement-calibrated digital twin. The comparison concerns complete deployments:
the saved provenance does not establish identical hardware or isolate the effect of kernel
bypass from the other implementation differences. The trace-to-load calibration is an
explicit scenario assumption.
1.4 – Research questions                                                                 4


   The title’s 5G/6G setting motivates analytics-assisted control in evolving mobile net-
works. The experimental claims concern the evaluated 5G case; application to a future 6G
deployment remains a direction for investigation. The scope is the observability pipeline
and the decision logic it supports, with physical closed-loop evaluation required to assess
deployment fidelity.
   The associated conference paper on sustainable multimedia delivery [7] develops a
broader 6G edge architecture in which digital twins support UPF orchestration, routing
and compute-resource decisions. It provides context for the present study’s focused
evaluation of measurement-grounded UPF realisation selection.


1.4     Research questions
Five research questions connect the component studies to the integrated evaluation. Chap-
ter 7 returns to each question and states the evidence supporting its answer.

RQ1 (measurement and attribution). How well can the profiling measurements support
     models of attributed upf power and performance, particularly near the operating
     boundaries relevant to selection? Chapters 3 and 5.

RQ2 (forecasting granularity). Does forecasting service-area demand directly improve
     prediction compared with aggregating per-station forecasts at a comparable model
     budget, and what decision errors remain? Chapter 4.

RQ3 (counterfactual evaluation). How can fitted upf models and transition measure-
     ments be combined to evaluate candidate realisations and derive model-dependent
     operating thresholds? Chapter 5.

RQ4 (shared-policy control). How do independent, shared-policy and centralised learn-
     ing designs compare under a common evaluation protocol, and what additional
     benefit is associated with a centralised critic? Chapter 6.

RQ5 (integrated evidence and validity). What does the complete pipeline establish
     about control performance within the modelled environment, how sensitive are
     the conclusions to its assumptions, and what further evidence is needed for physical
     deployment? Chapters 6 and 7.

1.5     Contributions
The contribution is the empirical development and evaluation of an integrated
measurement-to-control pipeline. Established learning methods are used to investigate
four connected design questions and produce corresponding experimental artefacts:

   1. Measurement-based deployment characterisation. Controlled profiling of the
      BESS/SD-Core DPDK and OpenAirInterface user-space deployments provides
      power and performance data under comparable traffic. Surrogate evaluation exam-
      ines held-out runs and the uncertainty relevant to decision boundaries (Chapter 3).

   2. Forecasting at the service-area scale. A fixed-budget comparison evaluates aggre-
      gation before forecasting against aggregation of per-station forecasts for the same
      delivered target. Model, cluster-count and partition comparisons assess the ac-
      curacy and cost of producing the demand signal needed by a site-level controller
      (Chapter 4).

   3. A common evaluator for candidate realisations. The digital twin combines
      fitted surrogates, load calibration and activation-cost accounting. It derives model-
      dependent operating points and provides a controller-independent interface for re-
      play and sensitivity analysis (Chapter 5).

   4. An evaluated comparison of controller designs. The study compares classical
      policies and independent, centralised and shared-policy learners. Additional runs
      retrain all learned controllers under one protocol and include a shared-actor ablation
      of critic structure. Repeated seeds, per-site analysis and sensitivity studies assess
      performance, variability and dependence on modelling assumptions (Chapter 6).

    Together, these contributions establish the utility of the inferred information within
the studied modelled conditions. Surrogate agreement with measurements and controller
performance inside the twin remain distinct forms of evidence. This distinction determines
both the interpretation of the results and the next steps toward physical operation.

1.7     Code and data availability
The work is organised across four repositories covering the profiling campaign, traffic

                            Related Work and Positioning
This chapter reviews the literature underlying the four pipeline stages introduced in Chap-
ter 1: metric attribution, traffic forecasting, digital-twin evaluation, and cooperative con-
trol. It distinguishes established methods from the questions addressed by their application
to UPF realisation selection, and examines how these methods can be validated. Detailed
comparisons of individual tools, model architectures, and placement algorithms are de-
veloped in the technical chapters and are not repeated here.


2.1     The measurement premise
The thesis begins from the claim that per-network-function energy cannot be measured
directly. That premise is not an assumption of this work; it is the position of both the
hardware documentation and the standards.
     Running Average Power Limit (RAPL) exposes energy counters for architectural do-
mains, including package, cores, uncore, and DRAM, rather than for software entities.
Systematic evaluations across platforms and workloads [1] map what each domain does
and does not cover, and comparisons of software power meters report material disagree-
ment between tools on identical hardware [8]. A value for one co-located network function
is therefore necessarily inferred from aggregate counters by an attribution model, not read
from a register.
     3GPP takes the same position normatively. TS 28.554 [2] defines energy-consumption
and energy-efficiency KPIs for network functions and states that the energy of a virtualised
network function cannot be measured directly and is consequently defined as an estimate.
The specification fixes the numerator, denominator and aggregation rules but deliberately
leaves the estimation method and its validation open. That open method is where this
thesis operates.

2.2 – UPF-level metric attribution                                                           8


2.2     UPF-level metric attribution
Attributing shared-host power to processes and containers is a mature field. Virtual-
machine and process-level estimators derive per-entity power from utilisation and cali-
brated models [9]; self-calibrating container meters such as SmartWatts learn the model
online from RAPL and performance counters [10]; Kepler exports workload-level energy
estimates in Kubernetes [11]; and EnergAt makes the allocation of shared and idle power
an explicit policy choice rather than an implementation detail [12]. A systematic com-
parison of CPU power-model families [13] finds that model choice and training context
strongly affect portability, which indicates that a per-function model is a calibrated artefact
tied to its host rather than a transferable constant.

The closest prior art. Bellin, Granelli and Munaretto [14] deploy Open5GS and
free5GC across bare-metal, virtual-machine and container configurations and combine ex-
ternal smart-plug readings with per-process estimates from the same power monitor used
here, reporting values for individual 5G-core processes including the UPF. This work
establishes prior application of per-NF power estimation to a 5G core. The remaining
questions concern comparative measurement and control: the authors report inconsistent
attribution in parts of their study, they do not compare data-plane realisations of the same
function under matched traffic, and they do not carry the resulting signal into control. Ear-
lier work by the same group [15] establishes the edge-deployment measurement platform
without the finer-grained analysis.

Method lineage. Estimating a quantity that is expensive or impossible to instrument,
from routinely measured proxies, is the established practice of soft sensing and virtual
metrology [16]. The mature literature is explicit about what such a sensor requires before it
can be treated as an observation: reference measurements, uncertainty, drift handling and
recalibration when operating conditions change [17]. This thesis adopts that vocabulary
deliberately. The method is not novel; the application to a UPF data plane, and the
discipline of validating it, are where the contribution lies.


2.3     Counterfactual digital-twin evaluation
Using a cheap learned model to stand in for an expensive evaluation inside a decision loop
is long-established as surrogate-based or metamodel-based optimisation [18]. The same
2.4 – Spatiotemporal mobile traffic forecasting                                                 9


literature carries the warning most relevant here: an optimiser will exploit surrogate error
if the model is wrong in a direction that appears favourable [19]. A controller choosing
between UPF realisations on inferred counterfactuals is exactly such an optimiser, which
is why Chapter 5 reports where the twin is queried outside its measured envelope rather
than only reporting the controller’s score.
     At network scale the same idea appears as the network digital twin. ITU-T Y.3090
[5] specifies requirements and a functional architecture, including data collection, models,
interfaces, and synchronisation, at a technology-neutral level, and the research literature
converges on similar blocks [20]. Surveys of the area [21] consistently identify the same
unresolved issue: the architectures say what a twin must contain, not how each physical
relationship is identified, calibrated and kept valid. The twin in Chapter 5 is therefore
not offered as a new architecture but as a concrete population-and-validation instance of a
known one.


2.4     Spatiotemporal mobile traffic forecasting
Forecasting cellular demand from historical traces is a well-populated field. Spatiotempo-
ral models of metropolitan cellular traffic [22] and convolutional citywide predictors [23]
established the problem shape: strong daily and weekly periodicity, spatial correlation
between nearby cells, and heavy-tailed peaks. The surveyed literature now spans classical,
convolutional, recurrent and graph-based approaches [24]. Nothing in the forecasting
component of this thesis is architecturally new: the models compared in Chapter 4 are
published designs [25, 26, 27, 28].
    Two things differ, and neither is an architectural claim. The first is the unit of prediction.
Most of the literature forecasts per cell or per grid tile, because the consumer is a radio-side
or planning application. The consumer here is a upf controller acting for a whole MEC
site, so the relevant signal is the aggregate over a cluster of base stations, and Chapter 4 asks
where aggregation should happen, before or after forecasting. That question is specific to
the deployment and is not, to my knowledge, settled in the cited literature. The second is
that forecast quality is reported in decision terms rather than only as error: what matters
for control is not the mean absolute error but how often the error changes the decision
(Section 4.13).
2.5 – Energy-aware control of network functions                                           10


    The comparison in Chapter 4 is nonetheless confined to one city and one dataset, so
the aggregation result should be read as evidence from a single deployment rather than a
general property of cellular traffic.


2.5     Energy-aware control of network functions
Runtime energy management of network functions is not new, and this thesis does not
claim it is. X-MAN adapts cloud-native network-function resources non-intrusively to
save power under service objectives [29]; GreenNFV profiles and schedules functions
against SLA constraints [30]; HADES integrates energy into placement and allocation
over heterogeneous infrastructure [31]. Model-based analytics over NFV infrastructure
telemetry has likewise been established for some time [32].
    For the UPF specifically, the empirical picture is developing quickly. Measurements
relate C-state configuration to UPF throughput, latency and power [33]; cloud-native 5/6G
stacks are shown to consume power non-proportionally with load [34]; realisation choice
is known to move the achievable performance envelope [35]. Most directly, Fuentes-
Gonzales et al. [36] study DPDK-based UPFs under varying CPU allocation and traffic
and derive energy-efficient management strategies, addressing precisely the cost of poll-
mode processing that motivates realisation switching here.
    These works occupy the space of energy-aware UPF control. What distinguishes the
present work is not the lever but the signal chain behind it: the decision is taken on an
attributed-power and QoS estimate whose accuracy is reported, a forecast whose error is
converted into decision terms, and a counterfactual evaluation of the realisation that is not
running.


2.6     Standards alignment and terminology
The four modules map onto roles the standardised architecture already anticipates.
NWDAF [4] defines analytics services, identifiers and consumers within the 5G core;
MDAF [3] provides the management-domain analytics counterpart; ETSI ZSM supplies
the closed-loop sequence of observation, analysis, decision, and execution into which the
modules fit [37, 6]. In every case the specification standardises the role and the interface
while leaving the sensing and decision internals to the implementation, which is the space
this thesis occupies rather than duplicates.
2.7 – What counts as validation                                                            11


Infrastructure-level analytics. The term Infrastructure Data Analytics Function
(IDAF) originates in research on energy-focused 6G management [38], where it names
an infrastructure-level analytics component. Unlike NWDAF and MDAF, IDAF is not
defined as a network function in the reviewed 3GPP specifications; its use here refers to
that research concept.

Augmented observability. The phrase “AI-augmented observability” and close variants
are used in adjacent fields. Here it denotes the application of four established methods—
soft sensing, forecasting, surrogate evaluation, and policy transfer—to quantities a 5G core
does not expose. The definition in Chapter 1 specifies the quantities inferred and their
roles in the control pipeline.


2.7     What counts as validation
The most consequential lesson from the literature concerns the argument this thesis can
support. A recurring risk in learned network control is to treat a controller’s success inside
a learned environment as evidence that the environment is faithful. The field does not
accept that inference. A policy optimised against a model can exploit the model’s errors,
so its score is partly a measure of those errors [19, 39].
    Accordingly, credible work in this area pairs learned-environment training with inde-
pendent evidence. Aurora trains under randomised simulated conditions and then evalu-
ates through a real networking stack [40]; Pensieve trains on traces and evaluates with real
clients [41]; Pantheon supplies calibrated emulation alongside live-path measurement so
that environment fidelity is testable rather than assumed [42].
    These examples distinguish validation of a policy from validation of its environment.
In this thesis, the controller results of Chapter 6 are reported as evidence of internal
consistency: the inferred signals are mutually coherent and sufficient to act on. They are
explicitly not reported as evidence of the twin’s fidelity to hardware. The component-level
evidence that does bear on fidelity is the held-out accuracy of the surrogates in Chapter 3,
measured against physical measurement, and the robustness checks in Chapters 5 and 6
showing that the conclusions survive changes to the modelling assumptions. The physical
measurements that would close the remaining gap are identified in Chapter 7.

3.1     Introduction
The User Plane Function (upf) is the component of a 5G core network responsible for rout-
ing and forwarding GTP-U encapsulated user traffic between the Radio Access Network
(RAN) and the Data Network (DN). Its power consumption is therefore directly coupled
to network load, and understanding this relationship is a prerequisite for energy-aware net-
work management and for meeting increasingly stringent sustainability targets in mobile
telecommunications.
     This chapter presents a systematic empirical study of two open-source upf implemen-
tations under controlled laboratory conditions. The first implementation, sd-core dpdk,
is the BESS-based user plane of the SD-Core project (bess-upf), which uses the Data
Plane Development Kit (DPDK) poll-mode driver to bypass the kernel network stack.
The second, usr-upf, is the user-plane component of OpenAirInterface, deployed with its
user-space application data plane: packets traverse the standard Linux networking stack
and are processed in user space through the socket API, so packet reception is driven by the
kernel’s interrupt (NAPI/softirq) path rather than by a dedicated polling loop. Traffic was
generated with a Keysight LoadCore emulator, and power was attributed per process using
the Scaphandre power monitor. Power is polled at 1 s and the merged record is emitted on
the 3 s cadence of the LoadCore export, which is the sample interval used throughout this
chapter and the interval Δ𝑡 of Equation (3.1).
     This chapter provides the UPF-level metric attribution stage of the pipeline. It estimates
quantities that a production core cannot natively measure. Hardware energy counters
(RAPL) report per-package power, not the power drawn by an individual network function.
Per-process power attribution is an established practice with a mature tool literature [10, 11,
12], and it has been applied to 5G-core functions including the upf [14]; what the campaign
below adds is a matched comparison of two data-plane realisations under identical offered

allocation [36]. As Chapter 2 sets out, per-upf attributed energy and QoS compliance
are not directly available from standard telemetry in an operational system. The profiling
campaign estimates those signals from controlled measurement, and the fitted surrogate
that closes the chapter turns them into a virtual sensor the rest of the pipeline can query.
The forecaster, the twin, and the controllers ultimately ground their decisions in the

3.2     Measurement Methodology

3.2.1    Testbed Configuration
Both upf variants were profiled using LoadCore traffic generation and Scaphandre process-
attributed power monitoring under comparable offered-load schedules. The saved cam-
paign records identify different Scaphandre exporters for the two variants and do not
establish a common physical host or identical CPU and NIC hardware. The comparison
is therefore between the recorded deployments; hardware equivalence remains unverified.
What differs between the two campaigns is the whole software stack under test, not a
single configuration switch. sd-core dpdk is the BESS user plane of SD-Core with the
NIC detached from the kernel and bound to a DPDK poll-mode driver, which additionally
requires hugepage backing and cores dedicated to the polling loop; usr-upf is the Ope-
nAirInterface user plane on the ordinary kernel networking path, which requests neither.
They are separate projects with separate codebases.

What this permits the comparison to attribute. The treatment in this campaign is
therefore the deployment, not the I/O model in isolation. A difference in power, delay or loss
3.2.2   Dataset                                                                         15


between sd-core dpdk and usr-upf is attributable to the pair (implementation, data-plane
architecture) taken jointly: no configuration in the campaign varies one while holding the
other fixed, so the contribution of kernel bypass cannot be separated from the contribution
of the surrounding implementation. Isolating the I/O model would require one codebase
built against both back-ends, which this campaign does not contain. Where the analysis
below explains a measured behaviour by the polling loop, as in the flat power profile of
Section 3.3, this is a mechanism proposed to account for the observation, consistent with
what poll-mode drivers are known to do, and not a contrast this experiment identifies on
its own. The surrogate models and everything downstream of them are unaffected by this:
they are fitted per variant and predict the behaviour of a named deployment, which is
exactly the quantity a controller choosing between two deployments needs.
     LoadCore injected downlink UDP flows with a fixed packet size of 1250 B at progres-
sively increasing offered loads. Each load step was sustained for a fixed duration to allow
steady-state measurements; the raw time-series data were then merged with per-second
power readings from Scaphandre. Figure 3.1 shows the full measurement architecture and
the monitoring path. Timestamp alignment of the performance and power logs produces
the surrogate training table described below.

3.5.1    Definitions

throughput delivered. Two variants are considered:

3.5.2    The Poll-Mode Measurement Paradox
The energy-per-bit form used here is the reciprocal of the data energy efficiency stan-
dardised for telecommunications equipment and mobile networks, which is defined as
delivered data divided by consumed energy in bit/J [46, 47]; 3GPP TS 28.554 [2] applies
the same construction at network-function scope, and defines the energy of a virtualised
function as an estimate. The reciprocal is valid only when numerator and denominator
share a boundary and an interval, so the boundary here is stated explicitly: the power
attributed to the upf process, the delivered downlink throughput, over one measurement
interval. Equation (3.2) then isolates the marginal energy cost of one additional gigabit by
removing the fixed idle overhead from the numerator. While this definition is appropriate
for interrupt-driven systems (where 𝑃idle ≪ 𝑃total ), it produces a misleading result for
poll-mode drivers.
    In sd-core dpdk, the CPU polls continuously regardless of load, so 𝑃idle ≈ 𝑃total ≈
0.821 W at all times. Consequently, 𝑃net = 𝑃total − 𝑃idle ≈ 0, and SECnet approaches
zero as throughput increases, superficially suggesting near-perfect efficiency. In reality,
sd-core dpdk consumes 0.82 W regardless of whether it forwards 0 or 5 Gbps.
    Table 3.3 and Figure 3.6 quantify this discrepancy at high throughput (> 1 Gbps).

  3.5.3                                     Implications for Energy Metric Selection
  For a fair cross-variant comparison, SECtotal is the more appropriate metric: it reflects the
  full operational cost of sustaining throughput 𝜆.
      One row of Table 3.3 must be read with care, and it is the row the headline comparison
  would otherwise rest on. Above 𝜆 50 the usr-upf throughput counter reports the load offered
  by the generator rather than the rate actually forwarded (Section 3.4), so at > 1 Gbps the
  usr-upf figure of 1.615 J/Gb divides power by traffic that was largely dropped. That
  violates the boundary condition stated in Section 3.5.1: numerator and denominator must
  cover the same delivered data. It is therefore not an efficiency measurement. It is reported
  here only to make the failure visible, and no cross-variant efficiency ratio is drawn from it.
  The defensible comparison is confined to loads below 𝜆 onset , where usr-upf delivers what
  is offered; there sd-core dpdk remains the more efficient variant per delivered bit, but by
  a margin that narrows sharply as load falls, which is precisely the regime the controllers
  exploit.
      Total power must also be considered in absolute terms: at idle sd-core dpdk draws
  0.82 W whereas usr-upf draws a median of 0.000 W (Table 3.1 and its footnote). The
  preferable variant therefore depends on the traffic load distribution in deployment.

3.7.3    Implications for Predictive Energy Modelling
The near-constant power of sd-core dpdk presents a challenge for regression-based energy
models: the target variable has a dynamic range of only 52 mW, approaching the noise
floor of Scaphandre. Models trained on DPDK data are expected to predict 0.82 W for
almost any input; the information content of the traffic features is minimal. Per-variant
models are therefore strongly recommended over a single pooled model.
     For usr-upf, the strong linear relationships (𝑟 > 0.8) between throughput, CPU,
and power suggest that simple linear or low-complexity tree models may suffice in the
unsaturated regime. Training should be restricted to the modelling envelope 𝜆 < 𝜆 env
unless the objective explicitly includes the saturated regime. The modelling envelope
𝜆 env and empirical low-load reference 𝜆 safe describe different aspects of the data; the
controller’s decision threshold is 𝜆 dec (Table 3.2).
     The rolling power features (power_watts_roll_mean_30s, power_watts_
roll_max_30s) achieve very high correlations with the power target (𝑟 > 0.98) and
must be excluded as predictors when the modelling goal is to predict power from non-power
observables.

20 ms-and-above bin is represented by 25 ms; this value is a coding convention, not
a measured maximum delay. The model’s 𝑅 2 = 0.968 therefore describes agreement
with the encoded summary, not recovery of the true delay in the high-delay tail. Finer
bins or packet-level measurements would be needed to assess that tail. For the 200 𝜇s
decision budget, samples dominated by that tail are clearly unacceptable, but the exact
delay magnitude remains unresolved.
    The throughput target carries the opposite caveat. Below saturation the upf delivers
essentially what is offered, so predicting delivered throughput from offered load is close
to reproducing an identity; the near-unity 𝑅 2 for that slot is therefore a consistency check
that the pipeline is wired correctly, not a measure of modelling skill. The informative
throughput result is the one at and above 𝜆 onset , where delivered and offered diverge.

3.9     Surrogate Models for the Digital Twin
The empirical findings of the preceding sections motivate a set of surrogate models: given
only an offered traffic load, they predict the upf’s power consumption, CPU utilisation,
delivered throughput, packet loss and one-way delay, without requiring a live deployment.
This section reports how those models were fitted and how accurate they are; the twin that
composes them into a simulator, including its architecture, switching-cost model, safety
semantics, and operating thresholds, is the subject of Chapter 5.
    Two terms are needed to read the results below. The models are fitted as a two-layer
stack per variant, mirroring the causal pathway load → performance → power. Layer 1
comprises four independent regressors mapping offered load to delivered throughput, CPU
utilisation, packet loss and downlink delay, using only TX-side controlled quantities as
inputs. Layer 2 is a single regressor predicting power_watts from those same inputs
plus the four Layer 1 outputs, which is what allows power to be predicted at inference
time without circularity, because the Layer 1 predictions are available before Layer 2 runs.
Section 5.2 develops the structure in full, including why each layer’s model family is
selected independently and what that implies for extrapolation.

3.9.1     Training Strategy
Three variant-specific models are trained to reflect the distinct operating regimes identified

        dpdk: all 4 388 DPDK samples. Included as an evidence baseline even though the
        near-constant power (52 mW range) makes the regression R2 inherently low.

    The 𝜆 env cut defines a restricted training envelope rather than a safe operating guarantee.
The usr_safe label is an implementation name for that subset, which includes samples
with loss. The full-range model is used for control evaluation because it includes the
wider loss and delay responses; 𝜆 safe remains an empirical reference and 𝜆 dec is the
separate controller threshold.
    For each variant and each prediction slot (Layer 1 target or Layer 2 target), three
model types are compared: Ridge regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosted
Trees. Hyperparameters are selected by 5-fold cross-validation on the training split, and
the model type is then chosen by test-set R2 .

Validity of the split. Two potential sources of optimistic bias were evaluated. The
train/test division is made at the level of individual samples, so measurements taken 3 s
apart within one sustained load step can fall on opposite sides of it; and choosing the
model type by test-set R2 means the reported score is also the selection criterion. Either
could inflate the numbers in Table 3.5.

by a grouped split in which no measurement run may appear on both sides, while every
other element, including features, model family, hyperparameter search, and random seed,
was held fixed. Across 220 runs and 8,781 samples:

3.9.2    Deployed models and scope of validation
The models used by the twin and by every downstream experiment are the ones fitted with
the row-level split; their metrics are those of Table 3.5. The grouped and nested protocols
above refit the same pipeline to estimate how optimistic those scores are. For full-feature
Layer 2 power, the sample-level refit gives 𝑅 2 = 0.286, 0.988 and 0.999 for DPDK, USR
full-range and USR restricted-range, and the grouped-run refit gives 0.241, 0.993 and
0.996. The refitted models are not substituted into the downstream experiments, so that
every controller result is computed with one consistent set of models.
    A complementary study withholds complete measurement sweeps and load levels,
selects models within the remaining runs, and evaluates the lite models through the twin’s
input adapter (Section 5.7.1). Because the deployed models were trained on rows from
every measurement run, this study validates the modelling procedure on unseen runs rather
than the deployed models themselves. Whole-range power accuracy and decision-region
safety accuracy are therefore reported separately.

3.11     Summary
This chapter characterised two 5G UPF implementations under controlled load conditions.
The principal findings are:

  1. DPDK is power-constant: total power varies by only 52 mW over the full 0–5 Gbps
     load range due to the CPU polling loop. This makes power prediction from traffic
     features statistically intractable.
3.11 – Summary                                                                            38


   2. USR is power-proportional but capacity-limited: power scales linearly from ≈ 0
      to ≈ 3.4 W, but packet loss becomes near-universal above 0.57 Gbps. The empirical
      low-load reference is ≈0.1 Gbps and does not guarantee loss-free operation.

   3. The SEC measurement paradox: the SECnet metric misleadingly reports near-
      zero energy efficiency for DPDK because the idle power is almost equal to the load
      power. Total SEC (SECtotal ) is the more operationally meaningful metric.

   4. CPU is the strongest legitimate predictor of power (𝑟 = 0.99 pooled), followed
      by delay-related features. Rolling power features are informative but constitute data
      leakage when used as predictors of power.

   5. Separate per-variant models are necessary: the two variants occupy distinct
      regimes with different predictive structures and cannot be modelled jointly without
      a variant indicator.

   6. The two-layer surrogate achieves high accuracy for usr-upf: the usr_safe
      power model reaches R2 = 0.999 (full) and R2 = 0.997 (lite), enabling power pre-
      diction from offered load alone without a live UPF.

   7. NetMob compatibility incurs minimal cost: the lite model, driven by normalised
      UL/DL loads only, sacrifices ΔR2 < 0.003 for usr_safe, making the twin directly
      usable with real-world traffic traces.

    Not all of these results travel equally far into the rest of the thesis. The digital twin
of Chapter 5 uses the lite dpdk and usr_full surrogates, because a safety-aware
evaluator must represent the saturated regime that usr_safe omits, together with the
usr-upf operating points of Table 3.2, from which it derives its own decision thresholds.
The SEC analysis and the correlation study characterise the two deployments and justify
the choice of total attributed power and per-variant models; they are not used again as
evaluation metrics. The restricted usr_safe model is retained as a low-load power
reference.

4.1     Introduction
In the augmented-observability pipeline, this chapter is the stage that makes the future
observable: the load a site will face in the next control interval, which is otherwise known
only after the decision has been taken. The digital twin of Chapter 5 requires an offered
load for each candidate upf evaluation. This chapter supplies that input through the
spatiotemporal traffic forecasting stage of the pipeline: a forecaster that predicts near-
future traffic for each of the 𝐾 geographic service areas into which a city’s base stations
are grouped.
     The interface between forecasting, calibration, and control is fixed explicitly. The
forecaster emits a dimensionless per-cluster load 𝑑.  ˆ Per base station the normalised demand
lies in [0, 1], but a cluster signal is the sum over its member base stations, so 𝑑ˆ is bounded
by cluster size rather than by unity. Over the deployed 𝐾 = 10 partition, the observed
maximum is 𝑑ˆ ≈ 5.8. The mapping to physical Gbps is the scenario constant 𝛼 owned
by the twin (Chapter 5, Eq. 5.1); it is applied after forecasting and never enters training.
Control decisions are made downstream, where controllers consume the forecast and its
error bar (Chapter 6). Forecasting is independent of physical load calibration, allowing
the same predictions to be evaluated under different deployment scenarios through the
interface in Section 4.5.
     The contribution of this chapter is an evidence-based answer to two design choices
that must be settled before a forecaster can be connected to the twin. The first is spa-
tial granularity: whether to aggregate base stations into 𝐾 service areas and forecast the
cluster signal directly (cluster-first), or to forecast every base station and sum the re-
sults. Section 4.11 answers this with a controlled ablation and finds the cluster-first route
substantially better, because aggregation denoises sparse per-station traffic and creates
predictability. The second is model selection. Section 4.7 benchmarks six models, from


                                              39
4.2 – Data and Spatial Substrate                                                            40


a linear baseline to established spatiotemporal graph networks, under a common protocol
and finds that the field is tight: a simple, inexpensive model loses little to the more complex
alternatives. The reusable substrate behind both results is the cluster-first pipeline itself.
On this problem, the relevant predictability improves chiefly through aggregation, with
the inter-cluster graph adding a smaller further gain. Section 4.12 shows that geography
supplies deployable service areas rather than a forecasting advantage over random groups.
Forecast accuracy is therefore part of the system contract rather than a private modelling
metric: the twin sizes its anti-flapping band directly from the forecast error, so forecast
quality propagates into control behaviour (Section 4.5).
    The chapter proceeds as follows. Section 4.2 builds the forecasting graph from raw
NetMob traffic. Section 4.4 introduces the forecasting models. Section 4.5 states the
output contract consumed by the twin. Section 4.6 defines the evaluation methodology
and the skill metric. Section 4.7 benchmarks the forecasting models and selects one;
Sections 4.9–4.10 characterise the chosen model across cluster counts and traffic types;
and Section 4.11 validates the cluster-first decision itself as the capstone. Section 4.14
summarises.


4.2     Data and Spatial Substrate
Before any forecasting model is involved, three steps turn raw operator measurements into
a signal on a graph: the measurements are normalised, attributed to base stations, and
the base stations are connected into an adjacency graph. This section documents those
steps and characterises the result, because the properties established here — how sparse a
single station is, how fast correlation decays in space — are what the design decisions of
the following sections rest on.


4.2.1    Dataset and normalisation
Dataset. The forecaster is driven by NetMob 2023, the same source the twin’s NetMob
adapter targets: per-tile downlink traffic for Lyon over 77 days (2019-03-16 to 2019-05-
31) at 15-minute resolution (96 slots/day; 𝑇 = 7388 slots, one hour being absent at the
daylight-saving change), for the services Netflix, YouTube, and DailyMotion. The spatial
unit is a 100 m × 100 m tile; the Lyon footprint comprises 54 013 tiles covering 539 km2 .
The raw values are privacy-preserving aggregate indicators with no physical unit; this is
the structural reason the pipeline is normalised and the twin needs 𝛼.

4.2.2    From tiles to base stations: Voronoi attribution
NetMob reports traffic per tile, whereas the network is operated per site. Tiles are attributed
to sites with a Voronoi tessellation built from the 965 distinct gNodeB locations inside the
Lyon footprint (ANFR Cartoradio; co-located antennas are merged). The tessellation is
computed in a local metric projection and each cell is clipped to the city footprint; a tile is
assigned to the cell that contains its centroid, and a station’s load is the sum over its tiles.
The Voronoi cell is the standard zero-information approximation of a coverage area: in
the absence of antenna azimuths, powers and propagation data, it assigns every location
to its nearest site.
     Figure 4.1(a) shows the resulting cells. Their size varies by nearly three orders of
magnitude, following the site density: the median cell covers 0.34 km2 (34 tiles), the mean
0.56 km2 , and the largest peripheral cell 7.5 km2 (Figure 4.2(a)). Traffic is concentrated
where the cells are smallest: the load density of the central cells exceeds that of the
periphery by up to two orders of magnitude.

 4.3                                       Service-Area Clustering
 With 965 base stations, neither per-station control nor — as Section 4.11 will show —
 per-station forecasting is viable. The stations are therefore grouped into 𝐾 service areas,
 each of which is the catchment of one edge UPF site and one node of the forecasting graph.
4.3.1   Requirements and method                                                            45


4.3.1    Requirements and method
A partition into service areas must satisfy three requirements that follow from the deploy-
ment rather than from forecasting. (i) Contiguity: a service area is the set of cells anchored
to one user plane function, so it must be a single connected region. (ii) Compactness: the
distance from a site to its UPF bounds the transport latency, so areas should be geographi-
cally tight. (iii) Service independence: the partition describes infrastructure and must not
change with the traffic type being forecast.
    These requirements are met by SKATER [48], a divisive regionalisation method based
on pruning a spanning tree of the adjacency graph. A minimum spanning tree of the
base-station graph is built, with edges weighted by inter-site distance; removing an edge
from a tree splits it into two connected subtrees, so any set of 𝐾 − 1 edge removals yields
𝐾 contiguous regions by construction. The edges are removed greedily: at each step the
method cuts the edge that most reduces the within-region sum of squared deviations of the
site coordinates from their region centroid,

                                          𝐾 ∑︁ ∥︁
                                         ∑︁                ∥︁
                                SSD =          ∥︁x𝑖 − x̄ 𝑘 ∥︁2 ,                        (4.2)
                                         𝑘=1 𝑖∈C𝑘


subject to both resulting regions containing at least three base stations. The procedure
is deterministic and uses coordinates only, so requirement (iii) holds trivially. Because
each step splits exactly one existing region, the partitions are nested across 𝐾: the service
areas at 𝐾+1 refine those at 𝐾, which makes results at different 𝐾 directly comparable.
One implementation detail should be noted: distances and deviations are evaluated in
geographic coordinates (degrees) rather than in a metric projection, which at Lyon’s
latitude under-weights east–west separations by a factor of about 0.70 relative to north–
south ones.
     Two alternatives were considered. Plain 𝑘-means on site coordinates yields compact
areas but ignores the adjacency graph, so contiguity is not guaranteed; it is kept as a com-
parison in Section 4.12. An attributed spectral clustering that mixed geographic affinity
with load-magnitude and temporal-shape features was explored in an earlier iteration and
abandoned: the traffic-feature terms produced spatially scattered areas and singletons, and
made the partition depend on the service, violating requirements (i) and (iii).

4.4            Forecasting Models
Every model predicts, for all nodes simultaneously, the next 𝐻 = 4 slots (1 h) from
the previous 𝐿 = 96 slots (24 h): input (𝐵, 𝐿, 𝑁) of normalised loads (one feature per
node) plus, for graph models, the static graph (edge_index, edge_attr); output (𝐵, 𝐻, 𝑁),
4.5 – Outputs and the Twin Data Contract                                                 49


with 𝑁 = 𝐾 clusters at the cluster level. To compare forecasters without favouring any
paradigm, the benchmark of Section 4.7 spans, under one training protocol:

Naive baselines. Persistence repeats the last value, and seasonal-naive repeats the same
     slot 24 h earlier. These are the references against which the skill metric is measured.

DLinear [28]: a channel-independent trend/seasonal decomposition with two linear
     maps, used as a simple baseline for testing whether a deep architecture is needed.

GRU (no graph) : a per-node gated recurrent encoder. It is the purely temporal model
    and the natural graph ablation, since any graph model’s edge over it estimates the
    value of spatial information.

DCRNN [25]: diffusion-convolutional recurrence, with spatial mixing fused into a GRU
   cell over the given graph.

Graph WaveNet [26]: dilated temporal convolutions with a learnable adjacency.

AGCRN [27]: a node-adaptive GRU over an adjacency the model learns from data,
   ignoring the supplied graph. This tests whether a better graph than the Voronoi
   construction can be learned from the data.

GAT–GRU (in-house) : a spatial-first encoder applies a linear input projection and two
    graph-attention layers (4 averaged heads) at every historical time step. A shared
    per-node GRU then encodes the resulting sequence, and a linear head maps its final
    state to the four forecast horizons. The GAT layers use ELU activations, residual
    connections and layer normalisation. This is the ClusterSTGNN implementation
    selected by the benchmark harness; it is included as an in-house baseline, not as a
    proposed method.

    The graph models share the same coarse 𝐾-node graph; the GRU and DLinear ignore
it. All are compact reimplementations on a common harness: results reflect each architec-
ture’s inductive bias under identical data, budget, and protocol rather than its best-tuned
reference performance. This is the appropriate basis for a controlled comparison.

4.11              Cluster-First versus Per-Station Forecasting
The preceding sections chose a model (Graph WaveNet) and showed it forecasts cluster
load robustly across graph sizes and traffic types. These results rest on the decision
to forecast at the cluster level. The MEC deployment already supplies clusters, but it
remains necessary to test whether aggregating before forecasting improves accuracy or
merely reduces computational cost. This closing section tests that decision directly.
    Section 4.9 cannot answer that question because it scores each 𝐾 against its own
changing target. A controlled test instead fixes the delivered target (the 𝐾=10 cluster
signal) and compares two routes to it:

Route A (cluster-first). Aggregate to 𝐾=10 clusters; forecast the cluster signals directly.

Route B (per-base-station). Forecast all 965 base stations; sum the predictions into the
     same 10 clusters.

Both routes are scored on the identical 𝐾=10 target. The 965-station series sums exactly
to the cluster series, verified to floating-point error, so the comparison isolates when

4.13      From Forecast Error to Decision Error
Reporting the forecaster’s skill in normalised MAE leaves a practical question unanswered:
the controller does not consume the forecast as a number, it consumes it as a decision, by
comparing it with the threshold 𝜆 dec = 81 Mbps derived in Chapter 5. Under the declared
calibration 𝛼 = 1 the horizon-averaged MAE of 110.7 Mbps is larger than that threshold,
which invites the conclusion that the forecaster cannot resolve the decision it feeds. This
section shows why that conclusion does not follow, and reports the metric that does matter.

Which horizon is scored. The model is trained on 𝐻 = 4 steps, but the controller
consumes only the first. The two are not interchangeable:

and 3.1 % that select sd-core dpdk unnecessarily, which costs only energy.
    The band table explains the discrepancy. The MAE is dominated by high-load steps,
where an error of 212 Mbps is large in absolute terms but flips no decisions at all, because
both the prediction and the actual load sit far above the threshold. Near the threshold,
where a flip is possible, the error is an order of magnitude smaller (21.9 Mbps). Comparing
a load-averaged error with a fixed low threshold therefore overstates the difficulty of the
decision by roughly a factor of four.

What this does and does not license. It does not make the forecaster accurate: 6.6 %
of steps are decided on a wrong premise, and the 3.5 % unsafe-direction share is the
mechanism behind the residual QoS violations of the threshold controller in Chapter 6. It
4.14 – Summary                                                                             63


does establish that the appropriate accuracy target for this application is the decision-flip
rate near 𝜆 dec , not the aggregate MAE, and that hysteresis bands sized as a multiple of
the aggregate MAE (Chapter 5) are calibrated against the wrong statistic. This is why the
automatic band degenerates.

What the spatial control adds. Aggregating stations before forecasting improves pre-
dictability, but the partition control in Section 4.12 shows that geography is not the source
of that smoothing advantage. Random groups forecast more easily because they resemble
the city total. Geographic service areas are retained because they correspond to a de-
ployable place, and SKATER guarantees their contiguity. The spatial pipeline therefore
defines the quantity needed by control rather than maximising forecasting accuracy alone.


4.14      Summary
This chapter built the spatiotemporal forecaster that supplies the digital twin’s offered
load. The principal points are:

   1. Scope. The forecaster emits a dimensionless per-cluster load 𝑑:ˆ a sum of per-station
      values that each lie in [0, 1], so 𝑑ˆ is bounded by cluster size rather than by unity
      (observed maximum ≈ 5.8). Physical calibration (𝛼) and control are external. One
      trained model serves every capacity scenario.

   2. Cluster-first is a forecasting advantage at equal model budget. A controlled
      ablation on a fixed target shows that forecasting the aggregated cluster signal di-
      rectly reaches 0.21–0.23 skill, while forecasting all 965 base stations and summing
      is no better than persistence (≈ 0). The aggregation denoises sparse per-station
      traffic into a predictable signal. Geographic partitions define local service demand;
      random partitions are even smoother but do not correspond to deployable areas
      (Section 4.12).

   3. Spatial structure helps modestly beyond aggregation. On top of the clusters, the
      inter-cluster graph adds only a small gain (∼3% WAPE; graph models > no-graph
      GRU), and a model that learns its adjacency (AGCRN) does not beat the given
      Voronoi graph, indicating that the residual spatial signal at cluster level is genuinely
      small.
4.14 – Summary                                                                         64


  4. Compact models offer competitive accuracy. Six forecasters from linear to
     learned-graph span only ∼0.06 skill; Graph WaveNet gives the best accuracy–cost
     trade-off and a plain GRU is near-best, so the system is insensitive to forecaster
     sophistication. Graph WaveNet is recommended for the twin.

  5. The problem is well-behaved. Skill over persistence is near-constant across graph
     sizes (𝐾 = 4 → 100; rising raw WAPE reflects intrinsic small-cluster difficulty, not
     model decay) and holds across aggregate and sparse traffic types, so the findings are
     not specific to the Netflix trace; they remain evidence from a single city.

5.1     Introduction
Chapter 3 established, from controlled laboratory measurements, how two open-source
upf implementations behave under load: sd-core dpdk draws a near-constant 0.82 W
regardless of traffic, whereas usr-upf scales its power proportionally to load but begins to
lose packets from about 0.17 Gbps and is fully saturated above roughly 0.5 Gbps. Those
measurements are the raw material; on their own they do not yet constitute a tool a network
controller can query.
    This chapter describes the construction of the digital twin used for counterfactual UPF
evaluation. It is a measurement-grounded, controller-agnostic software component that
answers a single well-posed question,

      Given an offered traffic load and a candidate upf configuration, what would
      happen in terms of power, throughput, CPU, packet loss, delay, and QoS
      safety?

The twin receives load values from the forecaster and candidate actions from a controller,
then returns the predicted consequences of those actions. This interface keeps the pipeline
modular: the forecaster owns the load prediction, the twin owns the physics and switching-
cost evaluation, and the controllers own the policy that selects a realisation. The same
boundary is reflected in the implementation through a reusable software library with
separate interfaces for evaluating candidate actions and simulating sequential decisions.
     As the counterfactual evaluation stage, the twin combines the UPF-level surrogates
fitted in Chapter 3 with the calibrated load interface used by Chapter 4. Runtime control
between two upf realisations requires exactly this comparison: the controller observes
the outcome under the running realisation and needs an estimate of the outcome that
would follow from switching to the other one. For illustration within this chapter, the

rollout examples use a transparent hysteresis rule (Section 5.8); the learned controllers
and clairvoyant reference are developed in Chapter 6.

5.2     From Profiling to a Predictive Surrogate
Using a learned surrogate as a cheap evaluator inside a decision loop is established
methodology [18, 19], and its network-scale form is specified as the digital twin network [5,
20]; Chapter 2 positions this twin against both. The twin’s predictive core is the two-layer
surrogate fitted in Chapter 3, whose accuracy is reported in Section 3.9. This section
develops the structure those results assume, then turns to the design decisions specific to
embedding it in an online evaluator.


5.2.1    Two-layer structure
For each upf variant the surrogate is organised as a stack that mirrors the causal pathway
load → performance → power:

Layer 1 (performance). Four independent single-target regressors map the offered load
     to delivered throughput (Gbps), CPU utilisation (%), downlink packet loss (packets
     per interval), and downlink one-way delay (𝜇s).

Layer 2 (power). A single regressor maps the offered load plus the four Layer 1 outputs
     to power_watts.

    Because each Layer 1 slot is selected independently by cross-validated model search,
the chosen algorithm differs by target: the near-linear throughput–load relationship is best
captured by Ridge regression, whereas the strongly non-linear delay and loss responses
5.2.2   Which variants the twin uses                                                      67


near saturation are best captured by tree ensembles (Random Forest or Gradient Boosting).
This heterogeneity is a feature, not an inconsistency: forcing a single algorithm across
all slots would underfit the non-linear targets. One practical consequence, visible later
in Figure 5.2, is that tree-based slots produce piecewise-constant predictions and do not
extrapolate beyond the measured envelope, a property the twin must respect when it is
queried.


5.2.2    Which variants the twin uses
The profiling campaign trained three variants: dpdk, usr_full (all USR samples, in-
cluding the saturated regime), and usr_safe (USR samples restricted to the unsaturated
regime below 𝜆 env ). The twin exposes exactly two actions to a controller, DPDK and USR,
and maps them to trained variants as follows:

                      DPDK ↦−→ dpdk,          USR ↦−→ usr_full.

The choice of usr_full over usr_safe to represent USR is deliberate and central to
the twin’s purpose. A digital twin whose remit explicitly includes packet loss, delay, and
QoS safety must be able to represent what happens when usr-upf is pushed past its safe
operating point. usr_safe was fitted to a restricted throughput range below 𝜆 env , which
includes some samples with loss but omits much of the saturated response. Predictions
beyond that subset’s support are insufficient to characterise the full safety transition.
usr_full includes the wider observed loss and delay behaviour; its full-feature Layer 1
scores (Table 3.5 protocol) are 𝑅 2 = 0.998 and 0.968, compared with 0.86 and 0.52
for usr_safe. The power score is slightly lower (𝑅 2 = 0.988 versus 0.999), but the
full-range model is better suited to evaluating both acceptable and unacceptable operating
conditions. The restricted-range models are retained as a low-load power reference rather
than used in the action map.


5.2.3    Full and lite feature sets
Each variant was trained in two feature regimes. The full model accepts all seven offered-
load features (DL/UL kbits/s, TX packet counts, average packet size, and the L2/L3
overhead ratio) and targets laboratory or high-fidelity simulation. The lite model accepts
only the DL/UL load volume and is therefore drivable from datasets such as NetMob 2023
that expose only aggregate per-cell load with no packet-level detail. The twin loads the lite
5.3 – Twin Architecture                                                                     68


variant exclusively, because its online use case is driven by cluster-level load forecasts that
carry no per-packet observability. On the row-level power test of Section 3.9, the accuracy
difference is small: for the power model the lite–full gap is Δ𝑅 2 ≈ 0.024 for usr_full.

5.4.1    Normalised load to physical load
The forecasting stage emits, for each cluster, the sum of the normalised per-base-station
demand over the cluster’s members. Each per-station value lies in [0, 1], so the cluster
signal 𝑑ˆ is bounded by the cluster size, not by unity: over the deployed 𝐾 = 10 partition
it ranges up to 𝑑ˆ ≈ 5.8. A single global calibration constant 𝛼 converts it to a physical
offered load in Gbps:

                  𝜆 Gbps = 𝛼 · 𝑑,
                                ˆ     𝛼 = 1.0 Gbps per normalised unit.                (5.1)

The value of 𝛼 is a scenario parameter; it sets the physical scale at which the (otherwise
dimensionless) forecast is interpreted, and hence where a given normalised load falls
relative to the usr-upf operating points of Table 3.2.
    The calibration 𝛼 is a declared modelling assumption, not a recovered calibration. The
NetMob traces are dimensionless by construction, for privacy reasons, and the forecaster
sums per-base-station normalised demand across each cluster; there is consequently no
physical scaler that could be looked up or fitted. The value was therefore chosen by
examining a range of scales (Section 5.9) and selecting the one that yields the most
useful mix of operating regimes across sites. At 𝛼 = 1, one unit of summed normalised
demand corresponds to 1 Gbps of offered upf load, placing the 𝐾 = 10 clusters at mean
loads of 0.11–1.66 Gbps with peaks to 5.8 Gbps. At this scale the fleet contains both
sites dominated by sd-core dpdk (the central, heavily loaded clusters) and sites that
spend substantial periods in the usr-upf-favourable region (the peripheral clusters), so the
selection problem is non-trivial across the fleet. Smaller values push almost every site
into the usr-upf regime, where the best policy is close to static usr-upf; larger values
push almost every site into the sd-core dpdk regime, where it is close to static sd-core
dpdk. The same value is used by the controller work of Chapter 6, so that both chapters
describe one physical regime. Section 5.8 returns to the consequences of this choice,
which are substantial: because the profiled usr-upf data path saturates one to two orders
of magnitude below these loads, 𝛼 largely determines how much scope for energy-aware
switching exists at all.

                         is_safe = loss ≤ 𝐿 max ∧ delay ≤ 𝐷 max ,                        (5.3)

        with the default budget 𝐷 max = 200 𝜇s and loss tolerance 𝐿 max = 5 packets per

        a packet count rather than a ratio has one consequence. At the campaign’s fixed

        0.021 % at 𝜆 dec = 81 Mbps and 0.011 % at 𝜆 qos = 149 Mbps. A fixed count is thus

5.8.4    Headroom for energy-aware switching
The case for switching at all rests on the load distribution. Figure 5.5 shows that the offered
load is heavily right-skewed, but that under the calibration of Equation (5.1) only about
14 % of cluster-steps fall below the decision threshold. In other words, only on roughly
one step in seven is usr-upf both safe and cheaper than sd-core dpdk. The remaining
steps exceed the decision threshold, so the rule selects DPDK; not all of them exceed the
separate saturation onset 𝜆 onset .
    A clairvoyant reference evaluates realisation selection using the actual load at each
step instead of a forecast. In the reported replay it saves 6.7 % relative to static DPDK.
This provides a benchmark for the forecast-driven policies under the same calibration and
accounting rules. It is not a proven upper bound on all causal controllers: the reference
does not establish a globally optimal sequence under switching costs and within-step safety
constraints. Accordingly, ratios to its saving are reported as relative performance, not as
fractions of a certified optimum.
    The reason is physical rather than algorithmic. The profiled usr-upf data path first
shows loss at 𝜆 loss = 60 Mbps and saturates from 𝜆 onset ≈ 170 Mbps (Table 3.2); the
derived break-even and QoS points, 𝜆be = 91 and 𝜆 qos = 149 Mbps, sit inside that band.

forecast and two derived thresholds. The stateless threshold policy achieves a marginally
higher saving (4.9 %) at a noticeably worse unsafe rate (4.2 %) and twice the switching

5.10      Summary
This chapter constructed a measurement-grounded digital twin of the upf data plane from
the profiling results of Chapter 3. The principal points are:

   1. Scope. The twin answers “what would happen” for a given (load, action) pair; it
      is neither a forecaster (Chapter 4) nor a controller (Chapter 6), and is kept strictly
      controller-agnostic.

   2. Variant choice. USR is represented by usr_full, not usr_safe, precisely
      because a safety-aware twin must be able to represent the full observed response;
      the restricted usr_safe subset does not adequately cover the high-load regime.

   3. Two modes. A stateless physics mode and a stateful rollout mode share one surrogate
      core; the latter adds a portable switching-cost model based on a measured activation
      duration and an energy-spike scaling rule.

   4. Derived thresholds, with one deliberate exception. The energy break-even and
      QoS limit are swept from the surrogate rather than assumed, keeping the operating
      points consistent with the measured physics. The anti-flapping band is the exception.
      Deriving it from the forecaster’s own error as 2 · MAE, as the earlier prototype did,
      gives 221 Mbps at 𝛼 = 1, which is wider than the 81 Mbps decision threshold it is
      meant to surround; that clamps 𝜆↓ to zero and collapses the controller into always-
      sd-core dpdk. This rule, used by the earlier prototype, is therefore replaced by a
      band set directly: 20 Mbps here, oriented to energy. Chapter 4 identifies why the
5.10 – Summary                                                                            88


     old rule fails: an aggregate MAE is the wrong statistic to size a band around a low
     threshold.

  5. Switching cost is small; hysteresis is a QoS argument. The per-switch energy is
     9.5 mWh against roughly 205 mWh for a sd-core dpdk step, some 4.6 %. Anti-
     flapping is therefore justified by QoS rather than by energy: a controller that flips on
     forecast jitter spends part of each transition on the realisation that is wrong for the
     load actually arriving. The sensitivity study of Section 5.9 bears this out: the plain
     threshold rule is marginally cheaper than hysteresis at every calibration examined,
     and consistently less safe.

  6. Demonstration. Driven by a transparent hysteresis controller, the twin recovers
     4.8 % of sd-core dpdk’s energy, roughly 72 % of the 6.7 % saving of the clairvoyant
     reference, while remaining safe on 97.0 % of usr-upf steps, quantitatively exposing
     the energy–safety trade-off that learned controllers will later be trained to navigate.

  7. Calibration-dependent opportunity. Under the declared calibration 𝛼 = 1, the
     profiled usr-upf path saturates one to two orders of magnitude below the offered
     cluster loads, so only ≈ 14 % of steps admit a usr-upf decision at all and static usr-
     upf costs 2.5× sd-core dpdk. The load distribution and reference replay quantify
     the limited opportunity for low-power operation in this scenario. They motivate
     evaluating QoS at comparable energy as well as reporting absolute saving.

are put to use, and it adds the fourth stage. A controller at one site can observe only that

additional runtime signal, and the chapter tests whether it improves decisions.

6.2     Starting point: a single-site prototype
The work began as a single, self-contained prototype [49] that combined, in one repository,
a hand-fitted power model, an offline traffic predictor, and a ppo controller for one upf site.
It established the problem shape and the reward ingredients that survive into the present
work, and its limitations are precisely what drove the modular pipeline developed in the
thesis.
    Three of its design choices matter because they motivate the design of the forecasting,
modelling, and control components. First, its power and QoS model was fitted inside
the control repository from a bespoke measurement set; there was no separation between
the evaluator and the controller, so the physical model and policy were developed as a
coupled system. Second, its traffic forecast was a Keras Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
network trained offline on a single time series, its predictions exported to a column the
environment read as static data, not a live model in the loop. Third, it was single-site by
construction: one environment, one agent, one upf location.
    Quantitatively the prototype demonstrated the potential for energy-aware control:
across five independently trained seeds a feed-forward ppo controller reduced mean at-
tributed energy by 16.9% relative to always-on sd-core dpdk provisioning, at an equal
QoS-violation rate to a hand-tuned rule-based threshold. It also showed, through an ex-
plicit ablation, that a recurrent ppo policy did not improve on the feed-forward one, a
comparison repeated with the modular digital twin (Section 6.5).
    The prototype established the feasibility of forecast-driven realisation selection at
one site. The subsequent research separated physical modelling from policy learning,
developed spatially aggregated traffic forecasts, and extended the control problem to a
fleet of sites. The following sections evaluate the resulting controller designs.

6.3     Problem formulation and rule-based baselines
Decision problem. A fleet of 𝐾 = 10 heterogeneous MEC clusters, each serving an
aggregated traffic load derived from the NetMob Lyon traces at 15-minute resolution,
must at every step select per site between sd-core dpdk and usr-upf. sd-core dpdk
sustains a near-constant 0.82 W power floor but tolerates high load; usr-upf is far cheaper
at low load but has a narrow safe operating region and violates the delay/loss budget
above roughly 0.15 Gbps. Switching realisations incurs a measured activation energy and,
optionally, a cooldown penalty. The controller sees, per site, the current load, an eight-step
load history, a one-step-ahead forecast, and the recent QoS, energy, and switching state (a
14-dimensional observation).

What the action denotes. Selecting usr-upf at a site means bringing the usr-upf data
plane up on that site’s host and cutting the site’s traffic over to it, tearing down the
realisation that was serving. That is the reading the cost model encodes: the penalty of
Section 5.6 is an activation cost, namely driver and hugepage initialisation for sd-core
dpdk and container start for usr-upf, incurred over a measured duration during which the
incoming realisation is not yet forwarding. It is not the cost of steering traffic between two
realisations that are both already resident.
    Two limits follow, and every controller result in this chapter inherits them. First,
the profiling campaign of Chapter 3 measured each realisation in steady state and never
observed a live transition: the transition is therefore composed from a separate activation
measurement rather than observed end to end, and because that measurement was taken on
a different host at whole-socket scope, only its duration and its dimensionless burst ratio
are carried across (Section 5.6). Second, an operator who instead kept both realisations
resident and merely re-routed between them would face a smaller cost, with no activation
energy and no activation outage, so the numbers below are conservative with respect
to that deployment style rather than descriptive of it. In neither reading does a switch
reconfigure one program between two I/O modes: the two realisations are different projects
(Section 3.2), so the fleet is heterogeneous by construction.

6.5     Explicit versus Recurrent Temporal Information
The observation vector encodes time explicitly, through an eight-step load history. An
alternative is to let the policy network carry temporal state implicitly, through recurrence:
an LSTM policy that reads only the current observation and maintains its own hidden
memory. Two experiments bear on the choice, and they support the same design decision
with different strength.

The prototype’s comparison. The single-site prototype [49] tuned both policy classes
and compared five seeds each with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The feed-forward policy
was numerically better on every metric: energy 122.6 ± 1.5 Wh against 125.3 Wh, mean
SEC lower by 7 %, and violation rate lower by 45 %. However, no difference reached
significance (𝑝 = 0.63, 0.38 and 0.50 respectively). With five paired samples the smallest
achievable two-tailed 𝑝 is 0.0625, so the test could not have declared significance for a
difference of this size. Training cost was the one clear separation: roughly 690 s against
443 s.

This work’s ablation. The comparison was repeated on the current environment, five
seeds, 200 000 steps, holding every hyperparameter identical across the two policy classes:

                                    Feed-forward     Recurrent    Wilcoxon 𝑝

                Test reward           −827 ± 26    −1238 ± 187         0.063
                Energy (Wh)          174.6 ± 2.2   200.5 ± 14.0        0.125
                Training time (s)        106 ± 4      598 ± 88         0.063

    The separation here is much larger, but for a reason that qualifies it. Four of the five
recurrent runs converged to always-sd-core dpdk, with reward exactly −1321.4, identical
to the static policy, while no feed-forward run did. The recurrent policy did not lose
the comparison so much as fail to leave the trivial local optimum within the budget, and
required 5.6× the training time.

Interpretation. Hyperparameters were held fixed across policy classes to keep the
comparison controlled, but that choice favours the architecture they were selected for;
a recurrent policy given its own tuning budget might well escape the local optimum, as
the prototype’s tuned comparison suggests it can. The defensible statement is therefore
not that recurrence cannot work here, but that it did not pay for itself: at matched budget
6.6 – Limitations of independent per-site learning                                         99


it was markedly harder to train and never better, and under the prototype’s separate tuning
it was still not better. Neither experiment provides a reason to prefer it.
     Across both experiments the temporal observability the controller needs is at least
as well supplied as an explicit, inspectable feature of the observed state as it is learned
implicitly by an opaque recurrent policy, and it is far cheaper to train. This is the same
design principle the whole stack embodies: make the decision-relevant signal an explicit,
validated observable rather than an implicit internal quantity. The explicit history window
is retained in every subsequent controller, and the recurrent variant is not pursued further.


6.6     Limitations of independent per-site learning
A per-site controller, whether rule-based or learned, sees only its own site. That is a
real limitation once the deployment is a fleet of heterogeneous clusters. Some clusters
carry dense, persistent demand and generate abundant experience around the switching
boundary; others sit at low load for long periods and rarely enter the unsafe region at all.
An independent learner at a low-load site may encounter relatively few boundary-crossing
events, limiting the experience available for learning the switching decision. Sharing
training experience is one way to address this imbalance.
    Two multi-site designs represent the extremes. A single centralised ppo, observing the
concatenated 140-dimensional fleet state and emitting a joint 10-dimensional action, has
the information in principle but must learn one monolithic policy over a joint space; at a
fixed training budget it is fragile. In this work the centralised controller collapsed: on one
low-load cluster (c6 in the illustrated seed) it degenerated to near-always-usr-upf, driving
that cluster’s QoS-violation rate well above every other controller’s, and its across-seed
reward variance was an order of magnitude larger than any other controller (Figure 6.3).
The opposite design, an independent ppo per site (an ippo ensemble), is stable and trivially
parallel to train, but by construction cannot share what one site learns with another.
    Independent per-site training cannot transfer experience between sites. Shared-policy
learning provides that capability, although its practical value also depends on optimisation
and the training budget. The following comparison evaluates whether a shared actor
and centralised critic improve performance relative to the independent and monolithic
alternatives.

twin’s QoS and energy signals, and can act ahead of an excursion rather than after it. Both
controllers do degrade per site as 𝐾 grows: mappo from −0.556 to −0.813, hysteresis from
−0.572 to −1.183. This is the signature of a harder problem rather than of one controller
scaling and the other collapsing. mappo degrades roughly 2.4 times more slowly.

What the sweep does not establish. It compares mappo against the classical baselines
only, so the widening margin cannot be attributed to cooperation specifically. The para-
graph above credits history, forecast and QoS state, inputs that an ensemble of independent
per-site learners also has. An ippo ensemble might therefore track the same trend, in which
case the widening would be a benefit of learning at scale rather than of parameter sharing
between sites. Separating the two would require running the ensemble across 𝐾, which is
not done here: the ensemble trains one policy per site, so its cost grows with 𝐾 exactly
where the comparison would matter most. What the sweep does establish is narrower and
still useful: the ranking reported at 𝐾 = 10 is not an artefact of that partition, and it does
not weaken as the fleet grows. The cooperative-versus-independent comparison at 𝐾 = 10
in Section 6.7, where the ensemble is actually run, remains the only evidence in this thesis
that isolates cooperation.

Safety widens too. The classical controller degrades on QoS as 𝐾 grows, from 0.10 %
to 2.24 % unsafe steps, while mappo rises only from 0.05 % to 0.59 %: at one hundred
sites the cooperative controller is ahead by 37 305 reward units, over fifty times the seed
standard deviation, and violates QoS on roughly a quarter as many steps. The same
burstiness accounts for it. A band of fixed physical width guards against forecast error of
a fixed size, but the excursions it must absorb grow with 𝐾 while the band does not, so the
rule is more and more often on the wrong side of the QoS limit when a spike arrives. The
learned controller does not share this failure because it acts on the twin’s counterfactual
QoS signal rather than on the forecast alone.

6.10                  Synthesis: what the control results do and do not
                      establish
The controllers form a ladder, and each rung consumes more of the augmented-
observability pipeline than the one below it. The derived threshold acts on a single
inferred signal, the one-step forecast, and is brittle precisely because that is all it sees.
The single-site learner adds the twin’s counterfactual QoS and energy signals and an ex-
plicit temporal history, and improves reward and safety relative to the threshold at similar
energy. The cooperative fleet controller adds cross-site policy transfer, making one site’s
experience visible to another, and beats the best classical controller by a margin larger
than seed noise, at the lowest QoS-violation rate of any learner.
    The chapter draws on two sets of runs, and they answer different questions. The runs
of Sections 6.4–6.8 compare shared and independent learning against an energy-focused
classical reference: the hysteresis band of 20 Mbps returns to usr-upf as early as possible,
and the comparison is read mainly in energy and QoS-violation terms. They establish that
a shared policy outperforms independent and centralised learners at 𝐾 = 10, and that its
6.10 – Synthesis: what the control results do and do not establish                        114


lead over classical control holds across load calibrations and fleet sizes. The additional
runs of Section 6.9, carried out for the conference paper, focus instead on the combined
reward. Their classical reference is therefore the hysteresis band optimised for that reward
(50 Mbps), and they add a Shared-PPO controller to isolate what the centralised critic
contributes beyond the shared actor, together with sensitivity to surrogate calibration
and reward weights. Read together, the two sets support the same ordering: shared-
policy learning first, classical hysteresis next, static sd-core dpdk last. Absolute rewards
differ between the sets because the controllers were retrained and the classical reference
changed; the independent learners in the additional runs include one collapsed seed, so
the cooperative-versus-independent margin is best read from the first set.
     These comparisons evaluate complete controller designs. The threshold and learning
policies differ in observation content, policy class, and training, so their performance
differences do not isolate the causal value of any one inferred signal. The MAPPO–
IPPO comparison at 𝐾 = 10 supports the benefit of the shared-actor, centralised-critic
design relative to independent policies; the Shared-PPO ablation of Section 6.9 tests
critic structure while retaining a shared actor, and finds a modest reward advantage under
the deployed calibration. It does not isolate every optimisation or selection mechanism.
Controlled input ablations would still be required to attribute each improvement to a
particular observability mechanism.
     These comparisons assess control performance within a common model. They do not
independently validate the twin: every policy is evaluated against the same surrogates,
and the classical thresholds are derived from those surrogates. A policy optimised against
a surrogate can also exploit its errors [19]. Independent physical evaluation is therefore
needed to establish performance in deployment, as illustrated by learned network-control
studies [40, 42, 39].
     What the results do establish is internal consistency: the attributed power, the forecast
and the counterfactual QoS are mutually coherent, and are informative enough that a
policy acting on them makes systematically better decisions than rules acting on fewer of
them. This supports their use for control within the modelled operating conditions.
     The evidence that bears on fidelity is separate and lives elsewhere: the held-out
surrogate procedure against physical measurement (Sections 3.9.2 and 5.7.1), including
its poor resolution near the QoS boundary; the sensitivity of the conclusions to the declared
load calibration (Section 5.9); and the invariance of the controller ordering across fleet
sizes (Section 6.8). Two limits remain. The load calibration is a declared assumption,
not a measured constant, and the operating regime follows from it. And the evaluation is
6.11 – Summary                                                                            115


an offline replay, not a live deployment; the measurements that would close the gap are
identified in Chapter 7.


6.11      Summary
This chapter trained and evaluated controllers that act on the inferred signals assembled
in Chapters 3–5. The principal points are:

   1. Progressive use of inferred signals. The controllers are ordered by how much
      of the augmented-observability pipeline each consumes: a single-signal threshold
      rule, a single-site learner that adds counterfactual QoS and energy plus temporal
      history, and a cooperative fleet learner that adds one site’s experience to another’s.

   2. Cooperation improves performance. mappo attains the best test reward in both
      sets of runs. Against independent learners its advantage is concentrated on several
      low- and mid-load clusters, consistent with a benefit from pooled experience. In the
      additional runs, which focus on the combined reward, its margin over Shared-PPO
      (same shared actor, local critic) has a positive seed-bootstrap interval but changes
      sign in one alternative calibration, so the critic’s contribution is modest.

   3. Coordination structure matters more than capacity. The centralised policy,
      given the same observation content, is the worst controller in the study and the least
      stable across seeds; the result is consistent with difficulty optimising the joint action
      space under the study’s training budget, rather than proving an inherent limitation
      of centralised control.

   4. Safety–energy trade-off. Static sd-core dpdk remains the safest option in the twin,
      and the learned controllers trade a small amount of safety for a reduction in energy.
      The clairvoyant reference of Chapter 5 contextualises that saving, while static sd-
      core dpdk provides a safety reference. The critic comparison of the additional runs
      does not establish an energy advantage of mappo over Shared-PPO.

   5. Evaluation domain. Results concern offline replay of the Lyon traces through the
      measurement-grounded twin. Physical deployment remains necessary to assess the
      transfer of these policies to a live network (Section 6.10).

                                                  General conclusion

7.1     Synthesis
This thesis has investigated augmented observability as a means of supplying the infor-
mation required for energy-aware upf control. Physical profiling, traffic forecasting and
a digital twin provide complementary estimates of attributed power, future service-area
demand and the consequences of selecting an alternative upf realisation. Controller exper-
iments then test how those estimates can be used, including the effect of sharing policies
across sites. The resulting pipeline connects measurement and inference to a concrete
network decision while making the boundaries of the supporting evidence explicit.
    The contribution spans the complete path from the quantity to be estimated to the
decision that consumes it. The forecasting target is chosen at the service-area scale; the
twin exposes the assumptions governing power, performance and transitions; and con-
troller evaluation reports the resulting energy and QoS behaviour alongside the combined
objective. This connection allows the component models to be assessed in relation to their
use, as well as by their individual prediction scores.


7.2     Answers to the research questions
RQ1 (measurement and attribution). The profiling study shows that the recorded mea-
surements support useful models of attributed power and performance over the studied
operating range (Chapters 3 and 5). Power prediction and safety classification neverthe-
less require different judgements. A strong whole-range regression fit does not ensure that
the model resolves the QoS transition accurately. Boundary-focused validation exposes
false-safe and false-unsafe predictions, making this region a priority for further measure-
ment (Section 5.7.1). The models characterise the two recorded deployments, with their
implementation and hardware effects remaining combined.



                                           116
7.2 – Answers to the research questions                                                   117


RQ2 (forecasting granularity). The forecasting study establishes that target granularity
is an important modelling choice. At a comparable model budget, forecasting the cluster
aggregate achieves 0.21–0.23 skill over persistence, whereas forecasting individual stations
and summing their outputs does not improve on persistence (Section 4.11). Geographic
contiguity defines a meaningful local service area; the partition comparison shows that it
does not explain all of the predictive gain. The separate decision analysis finds that forecast
error changes 6.6 % of threshold decisions under its stated calibration (Section 4.13).
Forecast quality should therefore also be assessed at the decision it supports.

RQ3 (counterfactual evaluation). The digital twin supplies a common method for
evaluating candidate upf realisations, including activation delay and switching energy
(Chapter 5). Its operating thresholds follow from the fitted models, traffic scaling and
QoS budgets. They make the assumptions of the comparison explicit, but remain model-
derived operating points whose physical fidelity requires independent measurement. The
clairvoyant replay supplies a reference under realised load, without establishing a glob-
ally optimal switching sequence. The twin enables repeatable offline comparisons with
consistent accounting across controllers.

RQ4 (shared-policy control). The controller results favour shared-policy learning under
the evaluated protocol. In the multi-site comparison of Section 6.7, mappo outperforms
independently trained per-site learners by 141 reward units (unpaired bootstrap 95 %
interval [54, 220], Mann–Whitney 𝑝 = 0.002), winning on seven of eight seeds, with
the largest gains on mid- and low-load clusters. In the additional runs of Section 6.9, in
which all learned controllers are retrained under one protocol, mappo again obtains the
highest mean reward across eight training seeds. Its 82.9-unit advantage over Shared-PPO
has a positive seed-bootstrap 95 % interval of [31.0, 150.9], while the energy comparison
does not establish an energy benefit. The additional reward reflects a different balance of
attributed energy and model-predicted QoS penalties. Static sd-core dpdk remains the
lowest-violation reference, and Shared-PPO consumes slightly less mean energy. Relative
to the test-tuned hysteresis reference, mappo reduces the combined cost by 10.2 % under
the stated weights.
    In the additional runs, one failed ippo seed is retained, and its influence on the mean is
reported alongside the median and spread. The mean difference from ippo consequently
includes training instability as well as typical policy performance. The Shared-PPO
comparison examines critic structure within a shared-actor design; establishing that pooled
7.3 – Limits and future work                                                             118


experience specifically benefits data-poor sites requires a targeted data-budget or transfer
experiment.

RQ5 (integrated evidence and validity). The experiments demonstrate that the inferred
quantities can be combined and used for control within the studied modelled conditions.
Calibration analysis bounds this result: mappo ranks first among the six compared con-
trollers in eight of ten alternative surrogate calibrations, with reversals favouring sd-core
dpdk and Shared-PPO in different refits (Section 6.9.2). This is evidence of conditional
robustness within the recorded campaign. The refits do not constitute ten independent
physical validations.
    The fleet-size and load-calibration studies provide complementary evidence under
their respective protocols. The former compares mappo with classical control from four to
one hundred sites; the latter examines rule-based ordering across load calibrations. These
studies do not extend the critic ablation to new hardware or independent traffic sources.
Repeated training seeds quantify variability under the fixed evaluation protocol and test
trace. Physical deployment fidelity remains a separate empirical question.


7.3     Limits and future work
The principal limitation is the absence of live closed-loop evaluation. Controller perfor-
mance is measured in the same family of calibrated environments used to generate the
inferred power and QoS signals. The surrogates are grounded in physical data, but model-
based safety near the operating boundary is uncertain, and the exact deployed surrogate
artefacts have not been assessed against an independent measurement campaign. The
trace-to-load mapping is also a scenario assumption, and the recorded deployments do not
establish hardware equivalence.
    The next experimental step is a boundary-focused profiling campaign on documented
target hardware, followed by independent validation of the exact deployed models and end-
to-end measurement of the switching process. A subsequent closed-loop test should assess
whether the offline behaviour survives actuation delay, measurement error and changes
in operating conditions. Monitoring prediction error during operation would make the
reliability of the inferred information itself an observable quantity.
    Further controller studies should separate the benefit of pooled experience from critic
structure and optimisation variability through controlled data-budget and transfer experi-
7.4 – Concluding perspective                                                            119


ments. Broader evaluations should vary traffic geography and deployment hardware, and
extend the binary selection action to include horizontal scaling.


7.4     Concluding perspective
The contribution of this thesis is a measurement-grounded framework for making addi-
tional network information available to control, and an empirical account of its utility and
limits. Energy-aware upf selection demonstrates how attributed, predictive and counter-
factual estimates can be combined into a common decision process. The evaluated 5G case
provides a basis for extending augmented observability toward more autonomous mobile
networks, including future 6G settings, provided that the validity of the inferred informa-
tion is assessed alongside the performance of the controller that uses it. The associated
work on digital twins for sustainable multimedia delivery [7] places this direction within
a broader 6G edge architecture linking user-plane orchestration with communication and
compute resources.
